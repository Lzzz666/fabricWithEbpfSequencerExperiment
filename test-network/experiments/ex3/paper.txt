This chapter presents a comprehensive evaluation of our batch-based transmit sequencer
design within Hyperledger Fabric, which we evaluate in two variants: one in which each
batch carries full transaction payloads, and one in which each batch carries only
transaction hashes. We compare both variants against traditional Raft consensus.
We begin by outlining the experimental setup, including hardware configurations and workload parameters, then assess system throughput
and latency under increasing request rates to highlight where each approach excels or
falls short. Building on these initial findings, we perform a time series analysis to capture
how performance evolves over extended periods of sustained load, identifying bottlenecks
at different stages of the packet handling pipeline.

4.1 Experimental Setup

All nodes were deployed on Google Cloud Platform (GCP) using e2-medium instances,
each equipped with 2 vCPUs and 4 GB of memory. The deployment consists of 3
peer nodes, 4 orderer nodes, and 1 sequencer node, all placed within the same GCP
region to ensure stable, low-latency communication.

We evaluated the performance of original Fabric (Raft) and NOPaxos(full transaction payloads and only hash) under various RPS levels by measuring TPS and latency. Transactions were
evenly distributed between two peers: peer1 and peer2 each received half of the total
target RPS. For the throughput experiment, all transactions committed over 60 seconds
were collected to compute TPS. For the latency experiment, each peer submitted
perPeerRPS * 10 transactions using a burst-based schedule—transactions were grouped
into 100 ms windows to maintain the target rate while reducing per-transaction timer
overhead.

Latency was analyzed across endorsement and commit stages to capture both transaction
submission delay and final confirmation delay. We report standard latency statistics
(P50, P95, P99, and mean).

Throughput:

Figure 2 shows that NOPaxos (hash) consistently achieves the highest throughput across
the tested request rates. In the low-to-medium load range (500-1500 RPS), NOPaxos
(hash) improves throughput by about 26.1% over Raft on average, and by about 26.2%
over NOPaxos (batch). Under higher offered load (>1500 RPS), the relative gap becomes
larger: NOPaxos (hash) outperforms Raft by about 97.6% on average and outperforms
NOPaxos (batch) by about 92.9% on average. Representative points include +44.5% over
Raft at 1500 RPS, +92.9% at 3000 RPS, and +139.8% at 4500 RPS.

A plausible explanation is that the hash-based variant reduces per-transaction overhead
along the ordering path (for example, serialization, transmission, and buffering), which
delays saturation as load increases. Once Raft and the batch variant approach saturation,
queueing and backpressure effects amplify throughput degradation, making the relative
advantage of NOPaxos (hash) more pronounced in the overloaded region.

4.2 Latency

End-to-end transaction latency (Ex3) is reported at P50, P95, and P99 for target RPS
from 100 to 1500. NoPaxos (hash) maintains the lowest median latency for most of the
range: P50 stays low until about 1200 RPS and then rises under the heaviest tested load.
NoPaxos-Batch follows the same low-latency regime until about 800–900 RPS, then degrades
sharply. Raft shows higher median latency at very low RPS, a mid-range minimum, and
then increases again as load grows. At 1500 RPS, comparing end-to-end latency in ratio
terms, NoPaxos (hash) has median latency about one-third of Raft’s (roughly a 2.8×
reduction) and about one-third of NoPaxos-Batch’s (roughly a 3.2× reduction). Tail
latencies tell a similar story: NoPaxos (hash) P95 and P99 are on the order of 35–40% of
NoPaxos-Batch’s at 1500 RPS (i.e., tails for the batch variant are roughly 2.5–2.7× higher),
while Raft’s tails remain substantially above NoPaxos (hash) as well.

We also isolate consensus latency (ordering only: total minus endorse and submit). At
1500 RPS, Raft’s consensus latency exceeds NoPaxos (hash) by roughly an order of
magnitude at the median (about 15×) and by several-fold at P95 and P99 (about 4× and
3× respectively), and exceeds NoPaxos-Batch by an even larger median factor (about
30×). By contrast, NoPaxos (hash) and NoPaxos-Batch differ only by small multiplicative
factors in this stage (median about 2×, P95 about 1.5×, P99 about 1.6×), i.e., they remain
within the same order of magnitude while Raft is far larger. This pattern is consistent
with hash-based ordering still requiring peers to correlate hashes with full transaction
payloads for validation and commit: reassembly and lookup largely offsets the lighter
on-the-wire representation in the consensus path, so consensus latency alone does not show
a large systematic advantage for the hash-only variant over full-payload batches. The
dominant end-to-end differences instead arise outside this narrowly defined consensus
interval (e.g., endorsement, queuing, and commit under load).

## Conclusions

We presented a sequencer-based batch transmission approach for Hyperledger Fabric. 
By aggregating client transactions into batches before the ordering stage, 
our design reduces the effective number of consensus/ordering rounds compared with the native Raft-based pipeline.

Experimental results show that this reduction translates into both lower end-to-end latency and higher throughput. 
The approach is also extensible to multiple sequencers, enabling load partitioning while mitigating single-point failures.
